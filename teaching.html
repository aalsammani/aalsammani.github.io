Teaching

Mathematics provides a language for describing complex systems and reasoning about them with precision. My teaching is designed to help students develop habits of thought that are rigorous, transferable, and grounded in first principles, whether they are studying mathematical analysis, probability, data science, or machine learning.

Teaching philosophy

Students should understand not only how a method works, but why it works, when it applies, and where its limitations begin.

Mathematical foundations. A regression, a numerical solver, or a neural network is a set of assumptions and a piece of mathematical structure before it is a line of code. I teach students to state those assumptions, follow the reasoning that justifies the method, and recognize the conditions under which it fails. A student who understands the mathematics is not confined to the examples in the textbook; that student can judge whether a familiar tool fits an unfamiliar problem.

From theory to computation to interpretation. My courses follow a consistent progression: a scientific question leads to a mathematical formulation, the formulation is carried out in computation, and the computation is interpreted in the language of the original question. Each step is taught explicitly. Students derive before they implement, validate before they trust, and interpret before they report, so that computation extends their mathematics rather than replacing it.

Independent thinking. The goal is a student who can formulate a problem, reason from assumptions, use computation intelligently, evaluate results critically, and explain the conclusions to someone else. Every assignment is designed with that student in mind, and the level of support decreases as the semester proceeds so that independence is practiced, not merely expected.

How I teach

Four principles shape my courses, from the order of topics in a syllabus to the design of a single problem set.

01Foundations before black boxes

Students meet the mathematical assumptions and structure behind a method before they rely on software to apply it. Definitions and central results come first; algorithms and library calls follow as consequences. When a student later reaches for a tool, the choice is informed by what the tool assumes and what it can and cannot guarantee.

Example: deriving the least-squares normal equations before running regression software, and examining the convexity assumptions behind gradient descent before tuning an optimizer.

02Computation with purpose

Python, R, MATLAB, numerical methods, and visualization are used to explore and extend the mathematics, never to replace it. Laboratory work asks students to implement a method from its mathematical description, validate the implementation against known results, keep the analysis reproducible, and interpret the output in terms of the problem it was meant to solve.

Example: implementing a regularized classifier from its objective function, then comparing it with a library implementation on real data.

03Research-informed learning

Examples and projects come from authentic problems in infectious-disease modeling, biomedical data science, computational neuroscience, and scientific machine learning. Parameter estimation in an epidemic model illustrates optimization; diagnostic screening illustrates conditional probability. Student research projects have contributed to scholarly outputs, including a peer-reviewed ICCSA paper on sleep-disorder prediction and a medRxiv preprint on ICU mortality prediction.

04Assessment for understanding

Assessments reward reasoning over memorization and formulation before calculation. A correct number without a correct setup earns little; a well-argued interpretation is part of every solution. Feedback is specific and constructive, and assignments are sequenced so that the scaffolding decreases while the expectation of independent judgment increases through the term.

Responsible use of generative AI

Use generative AI to support your learning, not to replace the thinking, skills, and responsibility your coursework is designed to develop. My student guide organizes that principle around three questions a student should ask before, during, and after using an AI tool.

Learning and growth

Does this use preserve the thinking the task exists to develop?

  • Attempt the task first.
  • Preserve the skill the assignment is designed to develop.
  • Verify AI-generated claims, calculations, code, and references.
  • Submit only work you can explain and defend.

Ethics and integrity

Is my own contribution honestly and clearly represented?

  • Disclose AI use when required.
  • Do not present AI-generated work as your own.
  • Follow course-specific expectations.
  • Agree on clear AI-use norms in collaborative work.

Awareness and safety

Is the output, and what I entered, safe to rely on?

  • Protect personal, confidential, proprietary, and identifying information.
  • Confirm course and assignment expectations before using AI.
  • Recognize that AI outputs may be inaccurate or biased.
  • Recheck guidance as tools and policies evolve.
