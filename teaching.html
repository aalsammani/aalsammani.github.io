Teaching

Mathematics is a language for describing complex systems precisely and reasoning about them with confidence. I teach so that students leave with habits of thought that are rigorous, portable, and grounded in first principles, whether the course is proof-based analysis, probability, or applied machine learning.

Teaching philosophy

My teaching in applied mathematics, statistics, data science, and machine learning rests on one conviction: understanding a method means understanding the mathematics that makes it work. Students who can state what a theorem assumes, why an algorithm converges, or what a probability model takes for granted are not limited to the examples they were shown. They can recognize when a familiar tool applies to an unfamiliar problem, and when it does not.

That conviction shapes how every course is built. Definitions and central results come first; worked examples and applications follow as consequences. In applied courses I anchor instruction in the underlying theory of optimization, linear algebra, probability, and inference, and I ask students to engage with algorithms at the level of their mathematical structure rather than as black boxes.

Understanding is also built by doing. Problem sets, computational laboratories, and project-based work give students repeated practice moving from a question to a formulation, from a formulation to a computation, and from a result to an interpretation that a scientist or clinician could act on. This is the working rhythm of applied mathematics, and students benefit from meeting it early.

Finally, I teach with the students in front of me in mind. Over more than a decade at institutions in Sudan and the United States I have taught students with widely varying preparation, and I adapt pacing, examples, and support to meet them where they are without lowering the standard they are working toward. Supporting students from groups historically underrepresented in the mathematical sciences is a responsibility I hold with conviction, and rigor and accessibility are, in my experience, complementary rather than competing goals.

How I teach

Four practices organize my classroom, from the structure of a syllabus to the design of a single assignment.

01

Theory before application

Every course follows a deliberate mathematical arc, so that computation is seen as an outgrowth of theory rather than a substitute for it.

  • In proof-based courses I walk students through the reasoning that produces a finished argument, not only the argument itself.
  • In applied courses every computational technique is preceded by the result that justifies it and the assumptions it depends on.
  • Students are asked to explain a method's failure modes as readily as its successes.

In the classroom

Definitions firstDerivations in classAssumptions made explicit

For example

  • Deriving the least-squares normal equations before running a regression.
  • Proving convergence of gradient descent for a convex loss before tuning learning rates.
02

Purposeful computation

Laboratory work in Python, R, or MATLAB is tightly coupled to each unit's mathematics, never an add-on.

  • A representative assignment: derive the gradient update for a regularized model, implement it, apply it to real data, and interpret the results in light of both statistical theory and the subject matter.
  • Students write code they can explain line by line, and they validate it against known results before trusting it on new data.
  • Reproducibility, documentation, and honest reporting of uncertainty are graded, not assumed.

In the classroom

Python · R · MATLABReal datasetsReproducible workflows

For example

  • Implementing a classifier from its objective function, then comparing it with a library implementation.
  • Fitting an epidemic model to case counts and reporting confidence in the estimates.
03

Research-informed examples

My research in infectious-disease modeling, computational neuroscience, and health analytics supplies the examples that show how far mathematical methods reach.

  • Optimization is illustrated through parameter estimation in epidemic models; conditional probability through diagnostic-screening problems familiar to students in health-related fields.
  • Capstone and independent-study projects are drawn from live questions, so that student work can lead to conference papers and preprints.
  • Questions of fairness, bias, and the social weight of modeling decisions are treated as part of doing applied mathematics well.

In the classroom

Epidemic modelsClinical predictionNeural signals

For example

  • A capstone on sleep-disorder prediction became a peer-reviewed ICCSA paper.
  • An undergraduate project on ICU mortality became a medRxiv preprint.
04

Active learning and assessment for understanding

Class time is for thinking, not transcription; assessment targets conceptual understanding rather than procedural recall.

  • Interactive learning modules, developed with support from a Scholarship of Teaching and Learning grant, let students test ideas before they are formalized.
  • Assignments build skill in a graduated sequence, and office hours are structured so that asking a hard question is the expected behavior.
  • Exams and projects reward a correct formulation and a well-argued interpretation over a memorized procedure.

In the classroom

Interactive modulesGraduated assignmentsConcept-focused assessment

For example

  • Short in-class prediction exercises before a new technique is introduced.
  • Project rubrics that weight problem formulation and interpretation alongside implementation.

Responsible use of generative AI

Generative AI is now part of how students learn, write, and code. Rather than ignore it, I integrate responsible and ethical use of AI into my courses, so that students learn to use these tools in ways that strengthen their own understanding and meet the standards of academic and professional integrity.

  • Learning comes firstAI tools may support study and exploration, but the mathematical reasoning, code, and interpretation a student submits must reflect that student's own understanding. If a tool did the thinking, the learning did not happen.
  • TransparencyStudents are expected to be clear about when and how AI assistance was used in an assignment, following the policy stated in each syllabus. Undisclosed use is treated as an integrity issue.
  • Verification and judgmentGenerated derivations, code, and citations can be fluent and wrong. Students learn to check outputs against theory, tests, and primary sources, and to take responsibility for what they turn in.
  • Ethics and societal impactIn data-science and machine-learning courses we examine bias, privacy, and the social consequences of automated decisions, because building models responsibly is part of doing applied mathematics well.
